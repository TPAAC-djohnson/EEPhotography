Honoring Black Women Who Documented Truth, Culture, and Humanity

Explore the Tribute

Introduction

Black women photographers have long used their cameras as instruments of memory, witness, and transformation. Through documentary, conceptual, and narrative image-making, they have preserved lived experience, challenged erasure, and made space for fuller truths about identity, culture, and community.

Featured Photographers

Carrie Mae Weems

Carrie Mae Weems is renowned for her narrative photography that explores themes of identity, family, and history. Through staged and documentary images, she interrogates the ways race and gender shape our understanding of the past and present.

Narrative photography, identity & history

Photographers of the Civil Rights Era

During the Civil Rights era, Black women photographers helped document marches, protests, organizing, and daily life. Their images preserved both resistance and humanity, building a visual memory of struggle, courage, and collective dignity.

Documentary photography, civil rights documentation

LaToya Ruby Frazier

LaToya Ruby Frazier uses photography and video to explore the impact of industry and economic decline on her hometown of Braddock, Pennsylvania. Her work documents community resilience and the ongoing struggles of working-class families.

Documentary storytelling about community & industry

Lorna Simpson

Lorna Simpson is a conceptual artist whose work combines photography, text, and installation to probe issues of race, gender, and identity. Her images challenge viewers to question assumptions and the ways photographs construct meaning.

Conceptual photography about race & gender

Deborah Willis

Historian and photographer Deborah Willis has dedicated her career to documenting Black culture and everyday life. Her images and scholarship have recovered countless stories of African American photographers and the communities they portray.

Historical documentation of Black culture

Ming Smith

As the first Black woman photographer to join the Kamoinge Workshop, Ming Smith is known for her impressionistic style and dynamic use of blur and light. Her photographs capture everyday life with poetic sensitivity and emotional depth.

First Black female photographer in the Kamoinge Workshop

Storytelling in Photography

Documentary photography does more than record events. It preserves social history, protects memory from distortion, and gives voice to people too often pushed to the margins. Through images of ordinary life, political struggle, beauty, grief, labor, and love, Black women photographers have expanded what history looks like.

Inspiration

“Photography is a potent medium for influencing the way we look at the world, the way we see ourselves, and the way we see others.”

— Deborah Willis

“Our responsibility is to create ourselves, to create an identity that speaks to who we are and who we want to be.”

— Carrie Mae Weems

Our Connection

Eve’s Eden Photography draws inspiration from these pioneering visual storytellers. Their work reminds us that photography is not only about beauty, but about witness, memory, presence, and the sacred act of being seen. This tribute honors their legacy by continuing a tradition of image-making rooted in depth, dignity, and story.
